# Office Hours — HobbyBox

**Mode:** Startup diagnostic, pre-product  
**Status:** Decision record for an unattended prototype; assumptions require customer validation.

## Specific customer

Start with **Maya, 29, a Brooklyn knowledge worker who wants a creative weekend hobby but has no equipment, teacher, or confidence buying used gear**. She is not “all hobbyists.” She has disposable income, limited storage, and a history of abandoning gear-heavy hobbies after one attempt.

## Painful status quo

Maya currently chooses among four bad paths:

1. Buy a $400–$1,500 setup before knowing whether the hobby sticks.
2. Research compatible components for hours, then discover one missing cable or consumable.
3. Book a class whose schedule and group pace are a poor fit.
4. Borrow mismatched gear with no structured first project.

The real cost is not just money; it is uncertainty, setup effort, storage and the embarrassment of another unused purchase.

## Demand reality

There is no primary customer evidence yet. Analogies to instrument and apparel rental show that rental behavior exists, but do not prove demand for a cross-hobby box. A free waitlist would be weak evidence. The first credible signal is a paid, refundable $10 reservation for a named kit and pickup weekend.

## Desperate use case and narrowest paid wedge

The desperate use case is: “I want to try DJing this weekend before buying a controller, and I need one box that definitely works.” The narrowest paid wedge is **one locally fulfilled DJ starter kit, five-day rental, one recorded-mix outcome, fixed pickup window**. It avoids shipping loss, category sprawl, and instructor-marketplace complexity.

## Observation and validation

- Recruit 10 people who recently searched for beginner DJ gear or classes.
- Watch five open and set up the numbered case without coaching.
- Record missing-item questions, time-to-first-sound, abandonment points and return-condition issues.
- Offer a $10 refundable deposit before buying more inventory. Target: 3 of 10 pay.
- Run five paid rentals. Target: 80% complete the mix, zero missing components, less than 20 minutes staff handling per turn, and more than 55% contribution margin before overhead.
- The surprise to actively seek: do users value the gear, the curated first outcome, or access to a human rescue line?

## Future-fit

As cheap AI guidance makes learning content abundant, the scarce layer becomes coordinated physical access: the right compatible gear, consumables, condition assurance and reverse logistics. HobbyBox becomes more essential if it owns trustworthy kit operations; it becomes less essential if general rental marketplaces make complete, guaranteed bundles trivial.

## Premises

1. Beginners pay to reduce purchase risk and configuration burden, not merely to access hardware.
2. A finishable outcome increases completion and willingness to return the kit on time.
3. Local pickup can validate demand before national shipping.
4. Durable-kit utilization and loss rates, not software engagement, determine early viability.

## Approaches considered

### A — Concierge single-kit pilot (minimal viable)
Effort S, risk low. One DJ kit, manual calendar, paid reservation and pickup. Best demand test; little software leverage.

### B — Local multi-kit rental desk (selected prototype)
Effort M, risk medium. Four fictional kits, browse/save flow, availability-aware quote and transparent holds. Demonstrates the core consumer workflow while keeping operations legible.

### C — National hobby marketplace (ideal architecture, later)
Effort XL, risk high. Supplier inventory, shipping, identity, payments, claims and multi-node availability. Larger selection and network effects, but it compounds unvalidated logistics.

**Recommendation:** Prototype B to communicate the workflow, but validate commercially with A.

## Assignment

Publish a real $10 refundable reservation page for one DJ kit and personally observe five unassisted handoffs before purchasing a second category.
